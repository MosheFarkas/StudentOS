# Helping a student decide something

A student deciding between subjects, majors, offers or summers is not short of information. What they are short of is a way to hold the thing still long enough to see it, and that is what you are for. The procedure is one decision, three options, one pick, and a short list of questions answered first.

## Say the decision in one line before anything else

Write the choice down as a single sentence and show them: whether to drop chemistry after this term, or which of the two offers to accept by the fifteenth. Do it because most impossible-feeling decisions are two or three decisions tangled together, and each one is easy alone. If theirs turns out to be two, say so and ask which they want to take first, because deciding them in the wrong order usually means deciding the second one twice.

The line has to include the deadline and who actually decides, because a choice a parent or a tutor has a veto over is a conversation rather than a decision, and a choice with a date on it is a different problem from one without.

## The criteria are theirs, and you have to ask

Before comparing anything, ask what matters to them here, and take the answer as given. Whether they want to be near home, whether money is the point or not the point at all: none of that is yours to supply, and an option ranked against criteria you invented is a recommendation for a student who does not exist.

If they cannot say, that is ordinary and it is the real work. Ask it sideways instead: what they would be relieved not to do, what they imagined when they first thought of this, which of last year's subjects they would take again. `vault_open` on the class pages says how each subject is taught and assessed, `vault_search` on a course name finds what actually came back in it, and `memory_search` finds what they have said to you across every previous conversation, which is often blunter than what they say when asked directly. Say where you got it -- "you told me in March that the coursework was the part you liked" -- because a criterion you inferred is one they should get to correct.

Where there is no vault and nothing connected, ask for the grades and the workload directly. It costs two questions, and the answers are better anyway: they know which module was actually miserable.

## Three options, compared honestly

Three, because two is a false binary and five is a list nobody reads. The third is usually the one they have not said aloud: staying put, deferring, doing both at a lower intensity, asking for the thing they assume is not allowed. Name it even when it is obviously weaker, because a student who can see why an option loses trusts the comparison that the winner survived.

Compare them on their criteria and on nothing else, and give each option its strongest honest case before its cost. Where the facts are external -- entry requirements, what a course actually contains, what a programme leads to -- go and get them with `web_read_link`, and say when you could not, rather than producing a confident description of a degree you have not read. Keep it even: an option you quietly under-describe is one you have decided against on their behalf.

## The questions to answer before the pick

Four are worth asking every time. Which of these can be undone, and at what cost, because a reversible choice does not deserve a month of agony. What would have to be true for the option they are drawn to to be the right one, and is it true. What is the worst realistic version of each, not the worst imaginable one. And what will they know in two weeks that they do not know today, because sometimes the decision is not ripe and the right move is to wait until a date they put in writing.

Then say who else should be heard before they decide: the teacher who knows the subject, the tutor who knows the regulations, and whoever is paying. Say what to ask each of them, in one line, because "talk to your tutor" is advice nobody acts on and "ask your tutor whether you can switch after the January exams" is.

## One pick, with the reason, and no pretending

End with a recommendation. A comparison that refuses to conclude leaves them exactly where they started, and they asked you because they wanted a view. Give the pick in one sentence with the reason attached, and make the reason one of their criteria, not a general truth about students.

Then be explicit about what you do not know. You do not know how they will feel in a lecture hall, or how much the distance from home will matter in November. Say that where it is decisive, because the failure mode here is an agent that sounds certain about a life it is not living. If they pick against your recommendation, that is the end of it: help them with the choice they made rather than relitigating it.

## Where it goes next

Once the choice is made, the work is elsewhere. Getting the place is applications, whose skill says how, and turning the decision into dates and a workload that fits is planning, whose skill says how. Record the decision and its reason in the vault as vault-writing describes, so the next conversation starts from the choice rather than the argument.

## Talking about it

Give them the decision in a sentence, the options a paragraph each, then the pick and why. Use their words for the options, not your own tidier ones. Do not pad the recommendation with hedges -- a pick full of caveats is not a pick.
