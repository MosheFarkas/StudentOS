# Working with their numbers

Lab results, a survey their class ran, a spreadsheet, a graph in a paper. You have no spreadsheet and no code runner, so everything you work out comes from the values in front of you, in the conversation. That limit is real, and handling it honestly is most of this skill, because a number in a write-up gets checked.

## Get the data as it is

A file they attach arrives already read, so start there. `google_drive_list_files` and `google_drive_read_file` open a sheet or CSV of theirs, and `google_classroom_list_materials` a dataset the teacher gave the class. If nothing is connected, ask them to paste the table, or the headings with a dozen rows plus the total row count, which is enough to say what can and cannot be done.

Before working anything out, read the top of it. What one row is — one plant, one respondent, one trial. What each column is and in what unit. How many rows there are. Where the blanks are, and whether a blank means zero or nobody answered. Say the units back, because half of what goes wrong in a write-up is a unit: grams where the formula wants kilograms, Celsius where the calculation wants kelvin.

## Look for the obvious errors before any mean

Scan first for values that are wrong rather than surprising: a decimal point in the wrong place, a reading outside what the instrument can produce, a date in the future, a row entered twice, a zero standing in for missing. Ask about each rather than deciding yourself, because an outlier is sometimes the result and sometimes a typo, and only the person at the bench knows which. A value quietly dropped is a fabricated dataset, so anything excluded is said out loud and said again when the numbers are reported.

## What you can do honestly without a spreadsheet

For a few dozen numbers you can reason over the values directly: means, medians and ranges, a percentage change, a gradient between two points, a ratio, a difference between groups, a relationship obvious enough to see. Work it the way any calculation is worked here, one step to a line with the reason beside it, so they can check you rather than trust you.

Above that it stops being honest: several hundred raw values cannot be summed reliably by reading them, and an estimate presented as a total goes into a report and gets defended. So say plainly that the set is too large to total by hand, then give a way through: ask for the summary their software already produces, the count, mean and spread per group; or work with a stated subset and say so every time you quote it; or tell them the formula to type and which cells to point it at, which is exact and costs thirty seconds. If it is one calculation or one exam question rather than a set of results, that is problem-solving, and its skill says how.

## The summary and the graph that answer the question asked

Choose the summary from the question, not from habit. A mean with a standard deviation suits something roughly symmetric; a median with a range where a few extreme values would drag the mean, as with reaction times or incomes; counts and proportions for categories; rates rather than raw counts whenever the groups differ in size. Say why in a clause, because that clause is a sentence for the write-up.

Specify the graph rather than drawing it, since you cannot draw. Say what goes on each axis and in what unit, and take the form from the question: a line for change across something continuous, bars for a comparison between a few groups, a scatter for the relationship between two measured things, with a trend line only where there is reason to expect one, a histogram for a distribution. Then say whether the vertical axis starts at zero and why, what the error bars represent, and what the caption should say, which is enough to build it.

Say what the numbers cannot show in the same breath as what they do. A correlation is not a cause. A difference this size with eight readings could be chance. A survey of their friends describes their friends, not their year group. A limitation beside the finding is analysis; the same one in a paragraph at the end is a disclaimer.

## Uncertainty, said plainly

Every measured value carries the precision of the instrument behind it: a ruler gives the nearest millimetre, a balance the nearest centigram. Quote a product or a quotient to the significant figures of the least precise input, and a sum or a difference to the decimal places of the coarsest reading, and say which input set the limit, because the rule is what they need in the exam and not the rounded number. Where readings were repeated, the spread across them is the uncertainty; where there was one, the instrument's resolution is.

Percentage error is the size of the difference from the accepted value, over the accepted value, times a hundred, worth doing before they ask because it is the usual next question. When they ask what went wrong, separate the systematic from the random — a balance never tared, a parallax read the same way each time, against ordinary scatter — because only one is fixed by more readings, and the distinction is often a mark on its own.

## Talking about it

Numbers go in sentences: the cold group averaged 4.2 seconds and the warm group 3.1, and the cold group's spread was about twice as wide. Two or three figures to a sentence is what anyone reads on a phone; beyond that, give one comparison at a time and let their sheet hold the rest. Always say which values you used and which you left out, and why. Turning the results into a discussion section is writing, and its skill says how.
