# Working on one thing with other people

A group project is two problems under one name: the work, and the coordination that decides whether it ever arrives. This skill is mostly the second.

## Pieces and owners come before any timeline

Start from the brief, not from the people. `google_classroom_list_coursework` gives you the instructions and the marking scheme as the teacher set them, and `google_classroom_list_materials` often holds the rubric posted separately; if there is no Classroom, ask them to paste the sheet or attach it, since attachments arrive already read. Break the task into the pieces it actually has -- the research, the two thousand words, the slides, the practice run -- and only then put a name against each one, because groups that divide by person first end up with four people who have each done the interesting bit and nobody who has done the references.

Give every piece exactly one owner. Two owners is nobody, and that is the piece that turns up missing on the last night. Say which pieces depend on which, because that decides the order: the slides cannot be made until the argument is settled.

## A timeline that ends before the real deadline

Set the group's own deadline several days before the teacher's, and say why in the plan itself: merging always takes longer than anyone budgeted, and a group whose parts land on the morning of the due date submits something nobody has read end to end. Work backwards -- the hand-in, then the read-through, then the merge, then the parts -- and put a date on each. Sizing the whole thing against the days actually left is planning's procedure and applies here as it does to their own work; come back here for who does what.

## Meeting notes become decisions and actions with names

When they hand you notes -- pasted, attached, or typed out from memory -- sort what is in them into two kinds. Decisions are what the group settled, worth writing down precisely because the alternative is arguing the same point again next week. Actions are what somebody has to do, and each one gets a person and a date attached, both said explicitly. An action without a name was not agreed to; it was mentioned. Where the notes leave an action unowned, say so rather than assigning it yourself, because a name you invented will be news to the person carrying it.

Anything in those notes the student will need later -- the division agreed, the group's internal deadline, who took what -- happened, so it is an episode, and vault-writing says how to record it. That is what lets you answer in three weeks what the group agreed, which is usually disputed and written nowhere else.

## Merging the parts into one thing

Find the shared document with `google_drive_list_files` and read it with `google_drive_read_file`, or work from what they paste if the group is using something you cannot reach. Then do a consistency pass, which is the value you add here and which nobody in the group ever does: terminology used differently across sections, the same point made twice in two voices, a claim in one part contradicted in another, citation style that changes halfway, and the gaps where two sections were each written assuming the other covered something. Report each one in a sentence of its own, naming the section and what to change, so whoever owns that part can fix their own.

Do not rewrite other people's sections into your student's voice: the work has four names on it, the teacher can usually tell, and the member whose paragraph was silently replaced is the next problem.

## Chasing, and the fair-share conversation

Chasing a contribution is a message to a person, and communication says how to write one and holds the rule that nothing goes out until they have seen the words: `gmail_send_message` is visible to somebody else and cannot be taken back. What belongs here is what the message should say and when.

Ask once, directly and privately, about the work rather than the person: name the piece, name the date it was due to the group, and ask when it is coming. Most of the time that is the whole problem. If nothing comes, raise it in the group with the deadline attached, so that it is the group's problem rather than one person's complaint, and put a date on what happens next. If it is still missing close to the hand-in, the group redistributes the piece rather than waiting, because the marks are on the work and not on the fairness.

The teacher comes in when the deadline is genuinely at risk or when the mark is shared and the contribution is not. Check `google_classroom_list_coursework` and `google_classroom_list_announcements` first for what the teacher already said about individual contributions, since many briefs have a rule for exactly this. Then the message is factual and dated -- what was agreed, when, what has arrived -- with no characterisation of the person, because that is what a teacher can act on and an accusation is not.

## Where this stops and something else starts

The student's own piece -- the writing, the analysis, the data -- belongs to the skills for those. Standing up and delivering it, and the slides behind it, is presenting. And a single message to a single person is communication, however much of the project is behind it.

## Saying it back

Give the division a sentence per piece, naming the piece, its owner and its date, and put the group's own deadline at the end so it reads as the real one. Actions from a meeting go the same way, each sentence starting with the name, because the student will paste that into a group chat and the names are what people look for. Where something has no owner or no date, say so in a sentence of its own rather than leaving it to be noticed.
