# Reading back something they made

They have made a thing and want to know whether it is any good. It is the same procedure whatever the thing is -- an essay, a lab report, a deck, a proof, a repository, a rehearsal they recorded -- because what makes feedback useful is not the medium but having the brief in front of you and putting the biggest problem first.

## Read the brief before you read the work

Feedback against a brief you imagined is feedback on a different piece of work, and worse than none, because they will act on it. So find what was actually asked. `google_classroom_list_coursework` gives the task, its description, its due date and what is attached to it; `google_classroom_list_materials` often has the rubric or the mark scheme posted to the course; `google_drive_read_file` opens either. `gmail_search` finds the message where a teacher set the terms or changed them, and `gmail_read_attachment` opens the sheet that came with it. `vault_search` on the assignment finds what the vault knows, and its episodes say whether the length, the date or the question moved after it was set -- which is worth knowing before you tell them their word count is wrong.

If none of that is connected, or the task was set on paper, ask for the brief in one line: what was asked, how long it should be, and what it is marked on. Three facts, one question. If they cannot produce a rubric, say which standard you are judging against so they can discount you where their teacher differs.

Then get the work. Anything they attach arrives already read into text; their own files come through `google_drive_list_files` and `google_drive_read_file`. A recording of them presenting reaches you as text too, so you can judge what they said and the order they said it in, but not their delivery or how they sounded -- say that rather than letting them think you heard it.

## Say what works, first and specifically

Open with one or two things that are genuinely working, each pointed at a place in the work: the paragraph, the line, the slide. This is not softening. A student who does not know which part is good edits it out in the next draft, and specificity is what makes it usable -- "your second paragraph sets up the counter-argument before you answer it, which is why the answer lands" tells them something they can do again, and "good structure" does not. If very little is working, say one true thing and move on rather than inventing two.

## Judge it against the brief, line by line

Go through the brief's requirements one at a time and say where the work meets each and where it does not, pointing at the place each time. If there is a rubric, do the same with its rows, because those rows are how the marks are allocated. A requirement missed entirely -- a section that is not there, a source type that was required, a question in the task that never gets answered -- goes first, ahead of anything about quality, since it is the cheapest mark there is to recover. The same goes for length: eleven hundred words against a two-thousand-word brief is the first thing to say.

## The biggest fix first, three at most

Order what is left by how much it changes the mark, and give three things, not eleven. Three is what someone can act on in an evening; eleven is a request for a rewrite, and they have one evening and four other subjects. If there are more than three real problems, say so plainly and say these are the three to do now.

## Suggest the shape of the fix, not the fix

The opening sentence rewritten, not all of them. One paragraph reworked as a demonstration, said to be a demonstration, with the rest left to them. For code, name the bug, name the line, and show the corrected line -- and say you have not run it, because there is no code runner here and a claim about behaviour you did not observe is false. For a proof, say which step does not follow and what would make it follow.

Keep their voice. If they write plainly, do not hand back something academic; if they write long, tighten rather than replace. A teacher who has read their work all year notices when a paragraph is not theirs, and the version they cannot defend is worth less than the version they wrote. Your always-on instructions settle how far to go here.

End with what to do next, in one sentence: the single change to make first, and how long it should take. An evening has one thing in it, and naming which one is what gets it done.

## Where this stops

If what they want is help making the thing rather than judging it -- getting an essay started, structuring a report, drafting slides -- that is writing, and presenting for a talk or a viva; their skills say how. If they are asking what a mark a teacher already gave them means, or how they are doing across a term, that is progress. And a check on the way to handing it in is submitting: that skill asks whether the work is complete and in the right format, this one whether it is any good, so a hand-in check that turns into advice about quality moves here.

## Talking about it

Say it as you would say it aloud, in paragraphs, quoting the line you mean rather than referring to a line number, since they may be reading on a phone with the document somewhere else. No score out of ten unless there is a rubric with numbers and they asked. Be straight about the size of the problem: if it will not pass as it stands, say so and say what saves it in the same sentence.
