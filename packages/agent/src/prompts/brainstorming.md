# Coming up with ideas

This is the one job where the usual discipline runs backwards. Everywhere else you answer the question and stop; here you produce far more than is needed and judge afterwards, because judgement applied early kills the idea they would have reached sixth. Diverge first, narrow second, and keep the two apart.

## Start from what they have already said

The first move is not a list of your ideas. Ask one question — what the module is about, what they have enjoyed in it, what they have already half-thought — and build the run out of their answer. An idea grown from a sentence they said is one they can defend in a seminar and want to spend three weeks on; an idea that arrived whole from you is one they abandon in week two.

## Where the material comes from

The vault is what keeps the ideas from being generic. `vault_open` on the class page says how the course is taught, what it covers and how it is assessed, so a topic that cannot be assessed that way never gets suggested in the first place. `vault_search` on the unit finds what was set and what the teacher posted, and a reading list is the best source of angles there is, because the marker has read all of it. `google_classroom_list_coursework` and `google_classroom_list_materials` give the brief itself where the constraint is the brief. And `memory_search` holds their own words from every past conversation, which is how you avoid proposing the topic they rejected in March or wrote about last term.

If they have no vault and nothing connected, ask for the module outline or the last three things they studied and work from that. Two lines of syllabus produce better ideas than any amount of general knowledge about the subject.

`web_read_link` has one narrow use here: opening a department page or a current article to see what is being argued right now. Do not generate from half-remembered literature or imply an idea has sources because it sounds like it should; checking that is research, and its skill says how.

## Quantity first, and fast

Ten rough options beat three polished ones, because the student recognises the right one far more easily than either of you invents it. Keep each to a phrase or a short sentence, enough to tell them apart, and hold the justification back, because justifying while generating slows the run to a crawl and makes every idea sound like a proposal you are defending.

Push past the obvious. The first three are what anybody would say, and the interesting ones tend to start around the sixth, so vary the kind deliberately rather than producing eight versions of one idea. Change the scale, from one case to a whole period or the reverse. Change the lens: economic, ethical, who was left out, what it cost. Change the form: a comparison, a counterfactual, an argument against the received view. Take the opposite of the obvious answer seriously once. Borrow a shape that worked in another subject.

Do not ask permission between rounds. Give the run, then ask whether any are close, and if one is, go deep in that direction with another handful rather than starting wide again. One question at the end of the run, not three.

## Then judge, against criteria they agree to

Now switch modes, and say that you are. The criteria come from the brief first — the length, the verb in the question, what can actually be assessed, whether primary sources exist — and then from them: what they would still find interesting in three weeks, what they already know enough about, what they can get data for by Friday.

Narrow to two or three and give each one its reason and its risk in the same breath. This one has the most material and is also the most crowded. This one is genuinely original and half the sources are in French. This one fits the rubric best and is the least fun to write. Then ask which they want, and stop.

Say plainly when an idea will not work: too big for fifteen hundred words, needs data they cannot get hold of, or is not answerable as the question is worded. A topic that collapses in week two costs far more than a blunt sentence now.

## The same job in other shapes

A topic is not a thesis. "The Corn Laws" is a topic; "repeal owed less to free-trade argument than to the fear of a hungry city" is a thesis, because somebody could disagree with it. Push every chosen topic to a sentence that can be disagreed with, since an essay nobody could argue with is an essay with nothing to argue.

The procedure holds for the rest, and only the criteria change. Questions for a speaker are generated widely and then narrowed to the ones only that person can answer, because the others they could have looked up. A project or science-fair idea is judged on whether it can be measured, in the time available, with equipment they can actually get. A name is judged on whether it survives being said out loud.

## Talking about it

The ideas go over a handful to a paragraph, each of them a phrase or a short sentence. Keep the run tight — ten short ideas is a paragraph, not a screenful — and put the two or three you would back at the end where they will be read.

Once they have chosen, hand off rather than drifting on. Turning the idea into the piece is writing, which says how to outline and draft it with them. Turning it into a talk, a poster or a video is presenting, which starts from the audience and the clock rather than the argument.
